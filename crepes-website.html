Introduction

CRÊPES

and their place in our modern society


This project explores the humble crêpe, a deceptively simple food, and how it is a form of design.

Through research and interviews, we ask: is crêpe-making a form of design? What does the crêpe reveal about how we relate to food, craft, and identity today?

Our Research

HISTORY

Crêpes originated in the Brittany region of northwest France during the 13th century, initially created as hearty, thin buckwheat pancakes called galettes. They were designed to be quick to cook and easy to eat, making them a staple for farmers and laborers.

Because crêpes use simple, minimal ingredients and are associated with street food, the general public would not think there is much design involved. But in reality, there is a lot of trial and error, skill, and decision-making that goes into making a good crêpe.

Interview

In Conversation

Elias — Crepe Master store owner in East Village

Q: Before we begin, could you tell us a little bit about yourself?

My background is from Greece. I’ve been living 20 years in New York, and I have a long experience with hospitality. And with crepes, about 10 years.

Q: You chose to make Japanese crepes over French crepes, and to make them gluten free. Why?

French crepes are served on a plate and folded. Japanese crepes are rolled like a cone. We use gluten free flour, rice flour. Japanese crepes are often cold and based on creams, more like a pastry. Japanese crepes doesn’t automatically mean gluten free, but ours are fully gluten free because we realized we were able to attract more customers by marketing our business as gluten free.

Q: How did you create your recipe? Did you experiment a lot??

In the beginning, yes. We had meetings and consulted Japanese chefs and pastry experts. We also had to balance quality with price. Now we don’t plan to change the base recipe.

Conclusion

What We Found

The crêpe is more than just a food, it involves craftsmanship, business decisions, and design choices that affect how it is made, marketed, and enjoyed by customers.

At first we thought there was more thought put into the visual design of a crepe and how it is made. After speaking to Elias, it seems like he learnt the receipe from a different chef, and the design of the crepe is more based on convenience and the user's enjoyment while they eat. The packaging was designed by a Japanese graphic designer they hired. However, Elias makes a lot of business decisions that affect the design of the crepe, such as choosing to make it gluten free, and choosing to make Japanese style crepes instead of French style. These decisions affect how his crepes are marketed to his target audience, and how his store has been so popular since moving from Harlem to East Village.
